\subsection{Introduction, Threat Modelling, and Applied Cryptography}

\subsubsection{Basic Definitions}

\definition{Adversary or Threat Model}{A system's security depends on the definition of the \underline{adversary}: who they are, what they can do (or their capabilities) and their determination (time, money, resources). This is the \underline{adversary} or \underline{threat model}.}

\separate{7}\definition{Asset}{An \underline{asset} is something that is worth protecting.}

\separate{7}\definition{Threat}{A \underline{threat} is a person or program that may try and access/steal your assets.}

\separate{7}\definition{Assumptions}{An \underline{assumption} is something you are relying on to be true/secure by default.}

\separate{7}\definition{Goals}{Your \underline{goal} should be in terms of what needs to be secure, and how long for.}

\separate{7}\definition{Controls}{The \underline{controls} are the things you can do to attempt to protect your assets.}

\separate{7}The CIA triad are the three main security properties, these are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{Confidentiality}{\underline{Confidentiality}: The outcomes of different actions in the system are only visible by authorised subjects.}
    \item \definition{Integrity}{\underline{Integrity}: Actions modifying the system can only be performed by authorised subjects.}
    \item \definition{Availability}{\underline{Availability}: Subjects can perform all actions they are authorised for.}
\end{itemize}
In addition to these properties, it is also important to consider additional properties, such as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{Authenticity}{\underline{Authenticity}: An entity is who they claim they claim to be.}
    \item \definition{Non-Repudiation}{\underline{Non-Repudiation}: An actor cannot easily deny that an action has occurred.}
    \item \definition{Context-Binding}{\underline{Context-Binding}: An action is valid only for a particular period of time, distance, location, and/or environment.}
\end{itemize}

\separate{7}\definition{Exploit}{An \underline{exploit} is a piece of code, a tool, or a technique that takes advantage of a flaw in software, hardware, or a network to cause unintended behaviour.}

\separate{7}\definition{Vulnerability}{A \underline{vulnerability} is a weakness or flaw in a system's design, implementation, operation, or human behaviour that an attacker can exploit to compromise security.}

\separate{7}\definition{Risk}{\underline{Risk} is the probability or liklihood that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm, loss, or negative consequences to an asset.}

\subsubsection{STRIDE Modelling}

\definition{STRIDE}{One method of classifying security risks is STRIDE, which was first developed by Microsoft. It is separated as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{Spoofing}{\underline{\textbf{S}poofing} is the act of using someone else's credentials to perform an operation and is a failure of identification and authentication.}
    \item \definition{Tampering}{\underline{\textbf{T}ampering} is the malicious modification of data and is a failure of integrity and authenticity.}
    \item \definition{Repudiation}{\underline{\textbf{R}epudiation} is the act of denying having performed an operation and is a failure of accountability and identification.}
    \item \definition{Information Dislosure}{\underline{\textbf{I}nformation Dislosure} is the exposure of information to unauthorised users and is a failure of confidentiality.}
    \item \definition{Denial of Service}{\underline{\textbf{D}enial of Service} is the act of denying service to legitimate users and is a failure of availability.}
    \item \definition{Elevation of Privilege}{\underline{\textbf{E}levation of Privilege} is an instance of an unprivileged user gaining privileged access and is a failure of confinement and authentication.}
\end{itemize}
This is a helpful model for structuring the analysis of potential system threats but it is not exhaustive or exclusive.}

\subsubsection{Applied Cryptography}

\definition{Encoding}{To \underline{encode} some given data, it is transformed from one format/domain to another. This is fully reversible, and does not provide any secrecy. The inverse procedure is called \underline{decoding}.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{Steganography}{A subtype of encoding is \underline{steganography}, where the existence of the secret message is concealed.}
\end{itemize}

\separate{7}\definition{Hashing}{To \underline{hash} some given data, it is transformed into a (usually) smaller, fixed-size. This is most commonly considered a one-way function. An example of this are SHA algorithms.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hashing algorithm works by taking a message \(m\) and applying a hashing algorithm \(h=H(m)\). \(h\) can then be sent securely and the recipient can then take \(h'=H(m')\) where \(m=m'\), and if \(h'=h\), then the message recieved is correct. 
\end{itemize}

\separate{7}\definition{Encryption}{Non-secret data (plaintext) can be \underline{encrypted} into ciphertext using a secret key. The output is \(Enc(m, K) = c\). The inverse procedure, \underline{decryption}, again uses a secret key to transform the ciphertext back to plaintext \(Dec(c, K)  = m\).}

\subsubsection{Cipher Examples}

\separate{7}\definition{Substitution Ciphers}{A \underline{substitution cipher} works by taking each letter in the alphabet and replacing it with another one. This was purportedly created and used by Julius Caesar. These are weak though and can be broken by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{Brute-Force Attack}{A \underline{brute-force} attack tries every possible key, which for substitution ciphers becomes trivial.}
    \item \definition{Frequency Analysis}{A \underline{frequency analysis} attack matches the ciphertext to the known frequency of English letters.}
\end{itemize}}

\separate{7}\definition{Vigenere Cipher}{Another historical example of a cipher is a \underline{Vigenere cipher}. This uses a sliding password, such that repeats of the same letter are coded differently. Attacks against this have to guess possible key lengths and then try all possibilities using frequency analysis. This is much harder to break.}

\separate{7}\definition{One Time Pad}{Another encryption method is using \underline{one time pads}. Here, you select a key as long as your message. Data is converted into binary and bitwise XORed with the key. To decode it, you then bitwise XOR the ciphertext with the key. This is considered theoretically secure as long as the key generation process is truly random, the key is never reused, and the key is kept secret.}

\separate{7}\definition{Stream Ciphers}{\underline{Stream ciphers} use a short secret key and a nonce - a use-once but public value - to generate a long pseudorandom sequence called a keystream. Only the short secret key must be kept private. We then XOR with the message and keystream.
\begin{itemize}
    \item To encrypt, you take the secret key \(K\) and the nonce \(N\) and feed them through a keystream generator. The resulting keystream \(S\) is then XORed with the plaintext \(p\) to produce ciphertext \(C\).
    \item To decrypt, the recipient takes the same secret key \(K\) and nonce \(N\), feeds them through the keystream generator and then XOR the result with the ciphertext \(C\) to produce the original plaintext \(p\).
\end{itemize}}

\separate{7}\definition{Block Cipher}{\underline{Block ciphers} transform a fixed-size block of plaintext into a ciphertext block of the same size, using a secret key. Encryption applies a keyed transformation to the plaintext block, and decryption reverses the transformation using the same key. An example of this is AES encryption. To encrypt longer messages, we use a mode of operation which specifies how to encrypt message blocks in sequence - this usually involves a nonce (initialisation vector).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{AES-ECB}{\underline{AES Electronic Code Book (ECB) mode} works by taking the plaintext, splitting it into blocks, and then encrypting each of them separately. This is not secure.}
    \item \definition{AES-CBC}{\underline{AES Cipher Block Chaining (CBC) mode} splits the plaintext into blocks and encrypts the first one. The result is then XORed with the second plaintext block before it is encrypted, and so on.}
\end{itemize}}

\subsubsection{Public Key Cryptography}

\definition{RSA}{\underline{Rivest, Shamir, Adelman (RSA)} is an algorithm where person A computes the product of two prime numbers \(n=p \times q\) and makes the value of \(n\) public. Person A then generates a public value \(e\) and a private value \(d\) using \(p\) and \(q\). \(e\) can then be used to encrypt messages to send to person A, and \(d\) used to decrypt them. This is secure based on the computational difficulty of factorisation, but with the rise of quantum computing, is becoming less secure.}

\separate{7}\definition{Digital signatures}{\underline{Digital signatures} enables others to check that a message was signed using a private key and has not been altered. To sign a message, the sender uses their private key, and then sends the message and signature to the recipient. To verify the signature, the recipient uses the sender's public key and compares the result to the message.}

\subsection{System Security}

\subsubsection{Analysing the Security of Systems}

\definition{System}{A \underline{system} is a set of components that interact to achieve a purpose. It generally consists of five components:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \definition{Subjects}{\underline{Subjects} are active entities with an identity and capabilities.}
    \item \definition{Resources}{\underline{Resources} are passive entities with an identity and properties.}
    \item \definition{Actions}{\underline{Actions} are interactions between subjects and resources.}
    \item \definition{Purpose}{The \underline{purpose} of a system is what it is used for, and so what security must preserve.}
    \item \definition{Boundary}{A \underline{boundary} defines what is inside (what you control), and what is outside.}
\end{itemize}

\separate{7}When considering the security of a system, we should consider four questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What matters?} An asset and a security property (e.g., confidentiality).
    \item \textbf{Who could cause harm?} An adversary with stated capabilities.
    \item \textbf{Why could they succeed?} A weakness and the assumptions it violates.
    \item \textbf{What changes the outcome?} Controls and countermeasures. What is the remaining risk?
\end{itemize}

\separate{7}\definition{Trust boundary}{A \underline{trust boundary} is where data or control passes between parties that trust each other differently (e.g., a phone to a ticketing service).}

\separate{7}\definition{Attack surface}{An \underline{attack surface} is every entry point an adversary can reach.}

\separate{7}\definition{TCB}{A \underline{trusted computing base (TCB)} is what must work correctly for the goal to hold. If any part fails, security fails.}

\separate{7}\definition{Reference monitor}{A \underline{reference monitor} sits between the subjects and objects. It recieves requests for the objects, compares them with the security policy, and if it passes, returns the requested objects. It is important when designing a system that this is always invoked, it is tamper-proof, and that the check is verifiable.}

\separate{7}\example{Hezbollah Pager Attack}{The \underline{Hezbollah pager attack} was an attack where Hezbollah ordered thousands of new pagers for members across Lebanon and Syria. This consignment was intercepted by agents in a supply chain attack and 10 to 20 grams of military grade explosive was packed into each device. These pagers were then delivered to Hezbollah and handed out to members. The pagers then recieved a code, buzzed, and exploded in pockets, on belts, or in faces of users.}

\separate{7}\definition{Kill chain}{It can also be useful to consider system security from an adversarial perspective. To do this, people commonly use the \underline{kill chain} model.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reconnaissance: Harvesting email addresses, conference information, etc.
    \item Weaponisation: Coupling an exploit with a backdoor into a deliverable payload.
    \item Delivery: Delivering a weaponised bundle to the victim via email, USB, etc.
    \item Exploitation: Exploiting a vulnerability to execute code on the victim's systems.
    \item Installation: Installing malware on an asset.
    \item Command and Control (C2): Setting up a control channel for remote manipulation of victims.
    \item Actions on Objectives: With \say{hands on keyboard} access, intruders can accomplish their original goals.
\end{itemize}}

\separate{7}\definition{Reconnaissance}{\underline{Reconnaissance} can be split into a few types: \underline{passive gathering} where attackers have no interaction with the target, \underline{active gathering} where they do, and \underline{open-source intelligence (OSINT)} where the attacker analyses data from publically available sources. They do this to identify potential attack vectors.}

\separate{7}\example{British Library}{The \underline{British Library attack} in 2023 can be split into the categories shown in the Kill chain:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reconnaissance: Hostile reconnaissance on the 25th-26th October was detected and blocked, but treated as a one-off.
    \item Delivery and Exploitation: Most likely privileged credentials obtained by phishing or brute force, then used on a remote-access server without MFA. No software exploit was needed.
    \item Installation and C2: The attackers hijacked the network's own administration tools, and deleted log files to cover their tracks.
    \item Actions on Objectives: A \say{historically complex} legacy network let them reach well beyond the entry point and roughly 600 GB was copied. The servers were then encrypted or destroyed.
\end{itemize}}

\subsubsection{Defence}

\definition{Asymmetry in Security}{\underline{Asymmetry in security} is the principle that finding a hole is much easier than securing all possible holes. However, an attack is a sequence of stages that are all opportunities to detect or stop it.}

\separate{7}\definition{Attack Trees}{An \underline{attack tree} is constructed such that: the root is the attackers' goal, the children are the different ways to achieve it, and the leaves are concrete actions. An OR node indicates that any of the children achieves the parent, while an AND node indicates that all children are required to achieve the parent. A set of leaves that satisfies the root is a complete attack.}

\separate{7}When designing a system, it is important to consider the following principles: minimise the attack surface, split your defence across many layers, assign users as few privileges as necessary, deny requests as a default reponse unless explicitly allowed, attackers will always take the weakest link, and you should always assume there will be a breach, so monitor and plan for recovery.

\subsection{Authorisation, Authentication, Accountability and Access Control}

\definition{Identification}{\underline{Identification} identifies who someone claims to be.}

\separate{7}\definition{Authentication}{\underline{Authentication} tests whether a person can prove their identity.}

\separate{7}\definition{Authorisation}{\underline{Authorisation} defines what a user is allowed to do.}

\separate{7}\definition{Access Control}{\underline{Access control} ensures that a user's authorisation is enforced on every request.}

\separate{7}\definition{Accountability}{\underline{Accountability} ensures that actions can be traced back to the user.}

\subsubsection{Authentication}

\definition{Authentication Lifecycle}{The \underline{authentication lifecycle} is separated into five steps. These are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Enrolment} binds an identity to a credential. This can go wrong if an attacker registers as you.
    \item \textbf{Authentication} requires the user to present their credentials for the verifier to check. This can go wrong after brute-force, phishing, or stolen password attacks.
    \item A \textbf{session} sets up a token to stand in for you on every later request. This goes wrong if a stolen cookie skips the login entirely.
    \item \textbf{Recovery} regains access when a credential is lost. This can go wrong if there are guessable security questions, or by exploiting the help-desk.
    \item \textbf{Revocation} removes access when people leave, or when credentials leak. This goes wrong if old codes still work.
\end{itemize}}

\separate{7}\definition{MFA}{\underline{Multi-factor authentication} can make a system more secure. The factors you can use are: something you know; something you have; and something you are.}

\separate{7}\definition{FAR/FRR}{No authenticator is perfect, we can judge their accuracy by measuring their \underline{false} \underline{acceptance rate} and \underline{false rejection rate}. 
Generally, lowering one raises the other.
For example, touch ID has a FAR of \(1:50,000\) and face ID has a FAR of \(1:1,000,000\).}

\separate{7}\definition{Similarity Score}{Each authentication attempt gives a \underline{similarity score}, not a boolean value. The threshold (visualised in \elemref{fig:Threshold Trade-off.}{Figure}\footnote{Image taken from \hyperref[cit:The Threshold Trade-Off]{Deparment of Computer Science (2026)}.}) turns it into a decision.
If we raise the threshold, then there will be fewer false accepts, but more false rejects, and vice-versa.}

\separate{7}\definition{EER}{The \underline{equal error rate} is where \(FAR=FRR\). 
This is good for comparing systems, but is rarely the right operating point.
The right setting depends on what each error costs.}

\noindent When hashing passwords, you should aim to use a slow hash function so that you can reduce the number of guesses per second, and therefore increase the time required to try all possible passwords.
For example, SHA-256 allows for 28 billion guesses per second, and so passwords can be guessed in a maximum of 2 minutes, whereas bcrypt only alows 9.5 thousand guesses per second, therefore increasing the time required to around 9 years.

\separate{7}\definition{Entropy}{\underline{Entropy} measures uncertainty in bits.
A secret chosen uniformly from \(S\) possibilities has \(H = \log_2 S\) bits.
\begin{equation}
    H(N,L) = \log_2(N^L) = L \log_2 N
\end{equation}
where \(N\) is the size of the alphabet, \(L\) is the length.}

\separate{7}\definition{Relay Attacks}{In \underline{relay attacks}, the attacker never breaks the cryptography.
Instead, they forward the challenge and the response so both ends belive they are closed.
To defend against this, you can introduce distance bounding where you time the challenge-response, or to require a user action.}

\subsubsection{Authorisation and Access Control}

\definition{Access Control Approaches}{\underline{Access control} can be implemented using one of the following approaches:
\begin{itemize}
    \item\underline{Discetionary (DAC)} where the owner decides who gets access. 
        Examples of this are Unix permissions, Windows ACLs, or sharing a Google Doc.
        It is flexible, but anything running as you, including malware, can give your data away.
    \item\underline{Mandatory (MAC)} where a system-wide policy decides and users cannot override it, even for files they create.
        Examples include government classifications or SELinux.
        It is rigid byt contains Trojan horses and careless users.
    \item\underline{Caoabilities} where unforgeable tokens are held by subjects. 
        It is good for delegation or distributed systems, but can lead to issues with revocation.
    \item\underline{Role-Based (RBAC)} where permissions attach to roles which are assigned to users.
        Joins, moves, and leaves change one assignment, rather than hundreds of permissions.
    \item\underline{Attribute-Based (ABAC)} where you decide the access from attributes of the subject, the object, the action, and the environment.
        This is good because it is fine-grained and context aware, but can lead to compications when trying to work out who can access a given file.
\end{itemize}}

\noindent An important principle is that users with a set access level cannot read up (you can only read an object if your clearance is \(\geq\) its classification), and cannot write down (write an object only if its classification \(\geq\) your clearance.)

\separate{7}Access control can fail when: the user is logged in, but nobody checks that this record is theirs;
admin pages are protected only by hiding the link from the menu;
or a role/price is in a hidden field, cookie, or JavaScript.

\subsubsection{Accountability}

When maintaining a system you should have methods for automatically logging: logins (successes and failures); MFA changes, password resets and recovery; role/permission changes; access to sensitive objects and denied requests; and administrative actions.

These logs should be protected by placing them off the host and making them append-only. 
They should also either use synchronised clocks, or there is no timeline, and should have an alert on patterns. 
They should also not allow any shared accounts.
