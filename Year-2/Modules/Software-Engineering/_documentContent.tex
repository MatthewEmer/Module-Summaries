\section{Software Engineering}

\subsection{The Software Development Lifecycle}

This lecture was taught by \hyperref[lecturer-nellybencomo]{Dr Nelly Bencomo}.

\separate{7}\definition{The Software Development Lifecycle}{The software development lifecycle is split into 6 stages: planning, analysis, design, implementation, testing and integration, and maintenance.}
This can be implemented in a number of ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Waterfall: Planned, sequential stages. Good: Visible stage boundaries. Bad: Feedback can arrive late.
    \item V-Model: Connects development levels to verification and validation. Good: Explicit assurance. Bad: Changes can be costly.
    \item Incremental: Deliver useful functionality in successive parts. Good: Early value. Bad: Integration and architecture.
    \item Iterative: Refine a solution through repeated feedback. Good: Learning. Bad: Potential rework.
    \item Spiral: Cycles driven by risk assessment and reduction. Good: Tackles high risks early. Bad: Expertise and overhead.
    \item Agile: Adaptive planning and frequent valuable increments. Good: Responsiveness. Bad: Sustained collaboration required.
\end{itemize}

\separate{7}The Agile Manifesto (2001) defines their priorities as people (individuals and interactions over processes and tools), software (working software over comprehensive documentation), collaboration (customer collaboration over contract negotiation), and change (responding to change over following a plan). 

It still requires engineering discipline though with requirements (clarify and revisit user needs), design (make and review architecture decisions), quality (test and integrate continuously), and feedback (inspect results with stakeholders).

\separate{7}Sprints: involves product owner, scrum master, and development team. 
Create sprints with product backlogs, that go into sprint planning. 
Then do a sprint review and potentially have a releasable product increment. 
There should also be regular checkups throughout the sprint to identify progress and issues.

\subsubsection{Extreme Programming}

\separate{7}\definition{Extreme Programming}{\underline{Extreme Programming (XP)} is built from five core values: communication, simplicity, feedback, courage, and respect.
Whilst doing this, we take engineering discipline into account by making small releases early to get client feedback, making continuous integrations to reduce surprises when merging teammates' work, and refactoring frequently to keep change affordable.

Generally, we split XP into 12 basic practices: the planning game, small releases, metaphor, simple design, test-first programming, refactoring, pair programming, continuous integration, collective ownership, on-site customer, 40-hour weeks, and an open workspace.}

\separate{7}\definition{Pair Programming}{\underline{Pair programming} is the practice of coding in pairs where one person is the driver and uses the keyboard, while the other is the observer who discusses with the driver and ensures the code makes sense.
The pair swap roles on a regular basis.
This provides learning benefits for less-experienced programmers.}

\subsection{Requirements I}

This lecture was taught by \hyperref[guest-pete-sawyer]{Professor Pete Sawyer}, taking content from his book: \hyperref[cit:Requirements Engineering]{Requirement Engineering: A Good Practice Guide}.

\separate{7}Requirements are important for a project as having wrong requirements can lead to the resulting software not being fit for purpose and errors can propogate and become costly to fix later.
It is important to validate early to ensure you have the right requirements.
As such, requirements statements become about communication. 
Stakeholders need confidence you understand what they want and developers need to know what to build.

\separate{7}\definition{Functional Requirements}{\underline{Functional requirements} give details about features the software provides.}

\separate{7}\definition{Non-Functional Requirements}{\underline{Non-functional requirements} give details about qualities or constraints for the project.
They can be dynamic (reliability, accuracy, availability, etc.) or static (maintainability, compliance, etc.).
They often act as design constraints such as budget or time.}

\separate{7}\definition{Stakeholders}{People or organisations with requirements are \underline{stakeholders}. 
Users perform the taret tasks, clients/customers pay for development, external/regulatory bodies ensure compliance, and other systems provide interaction requirements.
As such, it is good practice to identify and document the software's stakeholders.}

\separate{7}When trying to elicit requirements from stakeholders you can conduct interviews, observations, workshops, or focus groups.
You can also add requirements after the software has deployed through bug reports and feature requests.
UML models can also be useful to contextualise workflows and the organisation's architecture / stakeholders' requirements.

\separate{7}Verification ensures that requirements have been met.
To do this, each requirement should have a test case. 
This can be challenging for NFRs as they can easily become poorly defined.

\subsubsection{Introduction to Software Engineering}

\definition{ISP}{An \underline{ill-structured problem}, as defined by \hyperref[cit:The Structure of Ill Structured Problems]{(Simon, 1973)}, is: elusive, unbounded, not understandable, without end conditions. They require orientation and context, and have many stakeholders and many possible solutions.}

\separate{7}\definition{Good Problem Solver}{A \underline{good problem solver} needs the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Method}: Refers to a formal procedure for accomplishing a goal, typically independent of the tools used.
    \item \textbf{Tool}: An instrument or automated system for accomplishing something in a better way.
    \item \textbf{Procedure}: A combination of tools and techniques to produce a product.
    \item \textbf{Paradigm}: The philosophy or approach for building a project.
\end{itemize}}

\separate{7}\definition{Stakeholder Roles}{For this module, a \underline{stakeholder} will generally fall into one of three roles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Customer/Client}: The company, organisation, or person who pays for the software system. 
    \item \textbf{Developer}: The company, organisation, or person who is building the software system.
    \item \textbf{User}: The person/people who will actually use the system.
\end{itemize}}
